Picture two olive beds on a west-facing terrace, ten metres apart. One is planted into rotavated ground, watered on a daily summer clock and fed each spring. The other goes in behind a re-laid dry-stone wall, mulched with its own prunings, and watered only long enough to establish. Several summers on, the first still scorches at the leaf margins through August and cannot easily be taken off irrigation. The second has needed none for two years.

The difference is not the plants. It is the set of decisions taken before either tree arrived. This is the practical case for bringing **permaculture principles** into high-end Mediterranean landscape architecture: a garden designed as an ecological system gains value over decades — in soil, canopy and water resilience — where a garden designed as a decorative object depreciates and has to be topped up with money, water and labour indefinitely. The vocabulary comes from Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, and in its most usable modern form from Jessi Bloom and Dave Boehnlein's *Practical Permaculture*.

## 1. Read the Site Before You Plant It

*(Holmgren: Observe and interact; Catch and store energy)*

Conventional landscaping treats a site's difficulties as things to override — thin soil is trucked over, wind is fenced out, summer heat is answered with more irrigation. Every override becomes a recurring line on the maintenance budget. A permacultural approach spends the first season measuring instead, so each constraint informs the design rather than fighting it.

*   **Sun and stored heat:** where the midday sun actually lands in July versus January, and which existing walls and rock already hold and release warmth. This sets planting positions before a single species is chosen.
*   **Water and landform:** where runoff concentrates after a September storm, so the layout can slow, spread and infiltrate it where the ground allows — with a designed overflow route for the storm that exceeds capacity.
*   **The soil that is there:** Mallorca's red, clay-rich soils vary sharply in depth, texture and drainage even within one property. Soil pits and laboratory analysis per zone come first, then a palette matched to what is found — *Olea europaea*, *Pistacia lentiscus*, *Ceratonia siliqua* — not hundreds of cubic metres of imported amendment. Our [Crestatx](/crestatx-garden-design/) garden is built on this soil-first logic: the ground was regenerated in place rather than replaced.

## 2. Shelter Is Infrastructure, Not Decoration

*(Holmgren: Use edges and value the marginal; Integrate rather than segregate)*

In ecology, the seam where two systems meet — the *ecotone* — is where exchange concentrates. Permaculture's "use edges" principle takes this as licence to work boundaries rather than smooth them away. On many Balearic estates the most consequential edge is vertical and built, and the island already has the craft for it: the *marges* and *parets seques* recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.

*   **Thermal mass:** a stone wall of real mass stores solar gain through the day and releases some of it after dusk, moderating the microclimate in the few metres closest to it — enough, in the right position, to change what will grow there.
*   **Wind in the lee:** placed across the prevailing *Tramuntana*, a wall creates a downwind zone where wind speed and evaporative demand both fall. The effect is a gradient — strongest near the wall, fading with distance — not the fixed percentage marketing literature likes to quote.
*   **Permeable margins:** a near-solid wall throws a short, turbulent wake, so we detail walls with openings and back them with a planted layer that does the aerodynamic smoothing the wall alone cannot. At [Sant Llorenç](/sant-llorenc/), re-laid dry stone and centenary olives work as a single sheltering structure.

## 3. Plant in Layers That Shade Themselves

*(Holmgren: Use and value diversity; Apply self-regulation and accept feedback)*

The forest-garden model of stacked layers is usually drawn for temperate climates with seven tiers. On a semi-arid Balearic site the useful version is compressed to four, and its main job is not yield but self-shading: each layer moderates the conditions for the one below, so the ground plane is never left exposed.

*   **Overstory (6–10 m+):** *Olea europaea* and *Ceratonia siliqua* form the ceiling that filters the acute midday sun.
*   **Sub-canopy (2.5–5 m):** *Punica granatum*, *Ficus carica*, and citrus where exposure and drainage suit it — the productive tier.
*   **Aromatic shrub (0.8–2 m):** *Pistacia lentiscus*, *Myrtus communis*, *Teucrium fruticans* — evergreen mass, spatial definition and scent.
*   **Living groundcover (0–0.4 m):** prostrate rosemary, *Lavandula dentata*, *Phlomis italica*, covering the soil so it neither bakes nor blows.

This is close to the palette that replaced a thirsty lawn across the whole garden at the [Hotel Cabot Las Velas](/hotelcabotlasvelas/) in Puerto Pollença. One qualification matters near buildings: in the wildland–urban interface, planting inside the defensible space around a house is governed first by fuel continuity, clearance and access, following the applicable Balearic guidance — the layering is then adapted within those limits.

## 4. Close the Loop as Far as a Garden Can

*(Holmgren: Produce no waste; Use and value renewable resources and services)*

The last move is to route the outputs of one part of the garden into the inputs of another. Screened prunings — with any diseased or regulated material set aside — are chipped on site and returned as mulch, building the organic matter these soils generally lack. Routine runoff is slowed and infiltrated where the ground allows; the excess is carried off on a designed route rather than left to find its own way.

No estate landscape is ever truly closed: it still imports water in the establishment years, nursery stock and the occasional soil-test-directed amendment. What good design does is reduce the leak — turning a garden that exports green waste and imports fertiliser into one where most of the material cycles on site and the soil's own biology does much of the feeding. ## An Enduring Image, Cheaper to Keep

None of this compromises the look a client is paying for. A mature olive over a silver understorey, a warm stone wall holding the last of the evening heat, a floor of rosemary you can smell before you see it — this is the image the Balearic estate has always traded on. The argument here is only that the image is cheaper to keep, and gets better rather than worse, when the garden is engineered as a system first and composed as a picture second.

![Fig. 3: A garden's annual upkeep over twenty years — a decorative scheme held up with inputs, its cost climbing, versus a garden designed as a system, where cost can fall as soil, canopy and shade establish.](/images/diagrams/cl-fig-cost-over-time.webp)

A landscape designed only for the handover photograph slides into recurring correction. A landscape designed for succession deepens — in shade, soil and character — over decades, provided establishment, pruning and water management are maintained. The method does not remove the work; it moves it earlier and lets it compound.
